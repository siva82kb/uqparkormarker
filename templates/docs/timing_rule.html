The rule

A clip is removed if either of these is true:

  • two neighbouring frames inside it have timestamps more than {{ '%g' | format(limit) }} s apart (a gap), or
  • any frame inside it has the same timestamp as the frame before it (the sign of a catch-up).

Every other clip is kept and can be picked. Whole videos are never removed, only the affected seconds. No time is changed.

Example. A 60-second video in which the program was held up for 3 seconds.

Before the hold-up the timestamps are normal: clips are kept. The hold-up is one gap of 3 s: removed. The catch-up lasts about 8 s, with bunched and repeated timestamps: removed. After that the timestamps are normal again: kept.

That video loses about 9 or 10 seconds. The other 50 seconds can still be picked.

Does the rule remove the late frames?

The rule looks only for gaps and repeats. It never looks at how late a timestamp is. So lateness, measured as on the previous page, is a fair check of it.

For the rule

  • Simple. Two checks that anyone can repeat from the timestamp files.
  • No time is changed. Every time written out is a timestamp as recorded.
  • Few assumptions. It needs only this: a late stretch shows a gap or a repeat. It does not need the camera to run at a steady rate.
  • Checked. In five of six participants the latest kept timestamp was 76 to 128 ms late. In the sixth it was 211 ms.
  • Keeps most footage. Only the affected seconds go. No video is dropped whole.

Against the rule

  • Footage is lost. Between 2.5% and 12% of possible clips, and up to 24% of task clips.
  • Some tasks vanish. A short task that falls inside a late stretch has no clip left: 1 to 8 of the 91 tasks.
  • The sample is tilted. Removed seconds cluster where the program was busy, often as a task starts. Behaviour at those moments is under-sampled.
  • It removes good frames too. A repeated timestamp can come from a hold-up of one frame. The rule removes the second around it all the same.
  • Not a guarantee. One participant kept a clip 211 ms late. And the check itself gives the least a timestamp can be late.
  • Missing frames are not caught. Where the camera delivered fewer than 30 frames a second, a clip still has its full number of frames, so it covers more than {{ clip_seconds }} s of real time and plays back faster than life. In {{ subject }} this is {{ report.share_long_clips | share }} of the allowed clips; the longest covers {{ '%.2f' | format(report.longest_clip) }} s. The clip's two saved timestamps do show its true length.
  • Small errors stay. A timestamp can still be one frame off (about 30 ms), and the fixed delay between filming and stamping is not measured.

Other ways that were considered

WayWhat it doesForAgainst
Do nothing Use every timestamp as saved. No footage lost. Some clips would be seconds out against the sensors, with no sign of it.
Count frames Ignore timestamps. Take frame 900 to be 30 s in. Immune to late timestamps. Every session has 0.6 to 17.7 s with no frames, so the count drifts by that much.
Remove Chosen Never use a clip with a gap or a repeat. This page. Simple, changes no time, checked on six participants. Loses footage and some tasks. Tilts the sample.
Correct Work out when each frame was filmed from the frames after it, as in the lateness measure. Keeps all footage and all tasks. Assumes a steady camera with no lost frames, which the missing seconds contradict. Gives a limit, not the true time. Changes the times written out, so it must be validated first, for example against the jump landings.
Correct and report Correct as above, and save each clip's lateness with it. Most information. A reader can decide what to trust. Everything against "Correct", and more to explain.

"Remove" was chosen as the quickest safe answer for the first paper. The two correcting ways are not ruled out. Neither has been agreed or built.
